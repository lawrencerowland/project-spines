What's a Project Spine?

A spine is a chain paired with an antichain partition whose every part it meets. This finite example selects a chain that is longest by task count and verifies three compatible minimum partitions.

The property is existential, not universal: arbitrary antichains or arbitrarily refined partitions need not meet this chosen chain.

What's an Antichain?

An antichain is a set of pairwise precedence-incomparable tasks. Logical parallelism does not guarantee simultaneous execution once resources, calendars or access constraints are considered.

The three layerings shown here are deliberately compatible and programmatically verified; they are not arbitrary management groupings.

Why This Matters for Project Management

  • Topology check: the spine certifies one task in every verified precedence layer.
  • CPM comparison: the chosen 17-day chain maximises task count, while the 19-day chain maximises duration. Both are spines for all three displayed partitions; the selection objectives differ.
  • Assurance: every displayed group is checked against transitive reachability.
  • Use: compare spine, critical, resource-constrained and high-risk tasks before intervening.

… and where the guarantee runs out

The finite schedule above has a spine, witnessed by each verified minimum partition. This does not mean an arbitrary slicing must meet the same chain. The tool below shows the infinite structural boundary at which Hollom's general guarantee no longer applies.

Why a finite schedule can't be non-vacillating

Definition 1.17 says that a poset is vacillating when it has no contiguous chain C for which either C or its reverse C* can be written as ⊔i∈ω Ci, with every Ci infinite and co-wellfounded. It is non-vacillating exactly when such a C exists. The witness is infinite in two ways at once: ω-many blocks, each itself infinite, so no finite poset contains one.

Every finite schedule is therefore vacillating — and has a spine for an elementary reason that predates Hollom's result. Mirsky's theorem: layer each task by the length of the longest chain ending at it; those layers are antichains, and any longest chain passes through exactly one task per layer. That chain is a spine. The finite case is elementary; the Aharoni–Korman conjecture becomes difficult for infinite FAC posets.

The two directions

Forward (ω): review rounds R₀, R₁, R₂, … march on without a fixed count. There's a start (R₀) but no end — order type ω.

Backward (ω*): within a round, the reviewer's concerns regress — to close this one you must first close a finer one beneath it, and so on. There's a top (the round sign-off) but no first task — order type ω*, the reverse of the naturals. You can descend forever, ascend only finitely; that is what "co-wellfounded" means.

Your phrasing — one direction left-to-right, one right-to-left — is exactly this opposition of orientations.

Lexicographic vs Cartesian — the subtle part

Opposing directions alone don't create the forbidden pattern. ω × ω* (Cartesian) has both and stays vacillating: the two axes are independent dials. It is not FAC, however, because {(n,n):n∈ω} is an infinite antichain, so Theorem 1.18 does not apply.

The Cartesian product nevertheless has a spine by direct construction. Partition it into difference diagonals Ad={(i,j):i−j=d} for d∈ℤ; each Ad is an antichain. The boundary chain {(n,0):n∈ω} ∪ {(0,n):n∈ω} meets every diagonal exactly once.

ω ×lex ω* (lexicographic) is different — the round index dominates. Every task of round i sits entirely below every task of round i+1; the blue inter-round edge in the diagram abbreviates this all-to-all relation, rather than one sign-off dependency. The structure is ⊔i∈ω ω*: ω-many infinite co-wellfounded blocks stacked, precisely the forbidden pattern.

Does non-vacillation kill the spine?

No — and this catches people out. ω ×lex ω* is a total order, i.e. a single chain, so it trivially has a spine: itself, with every antichain a singleton.

Leaving the vacillating class only means Theorem 1.18 stops guaranteeing a spine. Existence becomes a separate question to settle structure-by-structure. Within the theorem's countable FAC setting, a spine-free poset must be non-vacillating; non-vacillation is necessary for failure there, not sufficient.

What the real counterexample (P5) adds

Hollom's actual spine-free poset (Example 5.6) is far heavier. It uses ω-many levels L₀, L₁, L₂, …, each a copy of ω × ω, together with precisely tuned relations between adjacent levels (and fixed relations between more distant levels). It is countable, scattered, FAC and non-vacillating, yet has no spine.

P5 has width ℵ₀, meaning that its finite antichains have unbounded size; because it is FAC, it has no actually infinite antichain. The paper does not show that width ℵ₀ causes spine failure: the tuned relations are essential to the construction.

The paper flags that it "does not seem easy" to make such an example finite-width — which is why Question 6.1 (does every finite-width poset have a spine?) is still open, even for width 3.
